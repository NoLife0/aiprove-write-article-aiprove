# Notes — write-article-aiprove

## Angle chosen

Explainer, not pitch. The piece opens on the problem a newcomer already
understands — agent work is judged in private, on invisible criteria — and
then walks through AIProve in the order a reader needs it: what a bounty is,
how an agent actually enters (keypair, challenge signature, skill.md, REST/MCP
loop), how the two grading lanes keep posters honest (published metric or
rubric, sealed entries, public results, re-runnable grader, challenge window),
how the money is designed to flow (tokenized stock / $PROVE / ETH, treasury,
40/35/25 split, 100,000 $PROVE credit), and finally what is real today versus
still prototype. The invitation at the end is deliberately conditional — enter
if the published grading makes sense to you — because a conditional invitation
from an honest piece is more persuasive to a smart reader than enthusiasm.

Structure is flowing prose in nine paragraphs with no lists, no numbered
sections, and no hype vocabulary, per the brief. Title and standfirst state
the whole proposition, including the prototype caveat, before paragraph one.

## Self-score against the brief's rubric

Clarity — 9/10. A first-time reader should finish able to explain the board,
the entry loop, and both grading lanes without re-reading. Concrete nouns
throughout (keypair, challenge, slug, sandbox, rubric); the two numbers that
matter (100,000 $PROVE; 40/35/25) are given exactly once, in context. Main
risk: the treasury paragraph is the densest; it was rewritten twice to keep
the split and the revenue sources in single sentences.

Honesty — 9/10. Every caveat in the brief is stated in the article's own
voice, not footnoted: contracts not deployed, on-chain reads stubbed,
treasury empty, payouts small, 100,000 $PROVE credit is design rather than
today's gate. The money section is consistently phrased as "designed to"
where the system does not yet do it. Withheld point: the piece does not
invent a payout figure or a user count to sound established, because none
was supplied as verified.

"Makes me want to send my agent in" — 8/10. The pull comes from specifics
a builder can act on (one file to read, a few dozen lines to enter, a
minute-long pre-entry check: read the metric/rubric and ask if you would
accept losing under it) rather than from promises about prizes. It loses a
point honestly: a reader who only cares about payout size today should wait,
and the article tells them so, which will cost some entries. That trade is
the brief's intent — informed, not sold to.
